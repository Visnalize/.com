![Indie developers editing app demo videos with AI generation tools on screen](/assets/covers/indie-developers-free-ai-video-generator-product-demo-videos.webp)

Apple's App Store Review Guidelines are blunt about this: preview videos can't include footage shot outside the app, features borrowed from a different version, or editing that implies the app does something it doesn't. A [free ai video generator](https://higgsfield.ai/ai-video) with built-in advanced features inside Higgsfield is becoming part of how indie developers put together demo videos, but only when every second of that video matches what's in the shipped build.

## Why Does This Matter So Much for Indie Developers Specifically?

It's not an abstract risk. Apple rejects preview videos that open with cinematic footage never captured from the app itself, or that show a device the listing doesn't support. Developer forums carry plenty of reports of AI-generated demos getting flagged because the interface on screen didn't match what reviewers found in the build. For a solo developer, that's not a minor inconvenience. A rejection sends you back to the end of the review queue, and that queue can eat several days you didn't plan to lose.

The risk lands harder here than at a bigger studio. A larger team usually has someone whose job is checking marketing assets against the actual build before anything goes out the door. A solo developer is coding, marketing, and hitting submit, often within the same afternoon, and that's exactly the setup where an AI-generated demo slips through without a second look.

## What Does Apple's Guidance Actually Say About Demo Videos?

Section 2.3 of the guidelines, Accurate Metadata, is direct: your app description, screenshots, and previews have to accurately reflect the app's core experience. Every claim on screen has to be demonstrable in the exact build under review. A demo video isn't creative territory the way a billboard ad might be. It's treated as a factual claim about a real product.

And the guideline doesn't stop applying after launch. The same section asks you to keep that metadata up to date with new versions. Ship an update with different functionality and leave the old demo video live, and you've now got the same mismatch the rule was written to catch, even though the video was accurate the day you first uploaded it. Keeping demo content current is something you do with every release, not something you check off once.

## What's the Right Way for Developers to Actually Use This Kind of Tool?

Use it to make real footage look better. Better pacing, cleaner cuts, narration that doesn't sound like you're reading off a script for the first time. What you shouldn't do is have it show a screen, a feature, or an interaction that isn't in the app someone downloads. Reviewers look for this gap between what's promised and what ships, and a rejection here costs real days you'll want back.

The safest workflow is boring and effective: record real screen footage from the actual build first, then let the tool polish footage that was already true. A developer tempted to have the tool invent a smoother version of a feature that isn't finished yet is building the exact mismatch Apple's rule exists to catch.

## What Happens When You Want a Polished Demo but Have No Production Budget?

This is where a lot of solo developers get stuck. You've built something that works well, but without video skills or a budget for one, your listing ends up carrying either bare screenshots or a shaky, unedited screen recording that doesn't show what using the app feels like.

That gap costs the most when the app's best qualities are things a screenshot can't capture: a smooth transition, a satisfying bit of animation, an interaction that just feels right. None of that survives a still image. An app that's a pleasure to use can end up looking flat in a listing, and you lose downloads from people who never got to see the part that would have sold them.

## What Are the Traditional Options for an App Demo Video?

Hire a freelancer, and you get polished, professional footage. Record a raw screen capture yourself, and you get something free and immediate but usually rough, missing the pacing and clarity that helps someone understand an app in the first five seconds of watching.

Each comes with a real cost, just a different kind. A freelancer's work looks great, but the price tag makes it hard to justify for every update, especially if you're shipping often. A raw recording costs nothing but time, and it shows. An app that's well-built can come across as amateurish next to a competitor with a more polished listing.
