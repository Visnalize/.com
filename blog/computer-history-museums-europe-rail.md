# The Computing Museum Road Trip: Bletchley Park to Helmond

![Cover image](https://images.pexels.com/photos/37148213/pexels-photo-37148213.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1260&h=750&dpr=1)

_Cover image by Ruben Boekeloo via [Pexels](https://www.pexels.com/photo/37148213)_

The Harwell Dekatron thinks slowly enough to watch. Orange digits crawl along its counting tubes in Block H at Bletchley Park, a machine first switched on in 1951 working through a sum your phone would have finished before you looked up, and the room goes quiet for it. It is the first exhibit on a route that links five computer history museums in Europe by rail, four countries and nine days from that block in Buckinghamshire to a Dutch town where every machine in the building is plugged in.

## The route, end to end

Bletchley Park and The National Museum of Computing sit on the same estate and sell separate tickets, which is the most common way to get this trip wrong before it starts. From there the line runs east to Helmond for the Home Computer Museum. Then Paderborn, for the Heinz Nixdorf MuseumsForum. It ends at the Musée Bolo, inside the EPFL campus in Lausanne.

Any one of them will show you machines. The route shows you the chain, in order: purpose-built codebreaking hardware, then the stored-program machine, then the mainframe as something a company could buy, then the micro on a teenager's desk. If you have ever written an emulator, you already know the distance between a service manual and the object it describes. Nine days of trains puts that distance back into your legs.

At three of the stops the machines are switched on and you are allowed to use them.

## Block H, Bletchley Park

Here is what nobody warns you about before you arrive. The Colossus in Block H is a rebuild, put together by volunteers working from a handful of wartime photographs and surviving circuit fragments, because the originals were broken up on official orders and the drawings burned. The Turing-Welchman Bombe across the estate is a rebuild too, assembled over more than a decade by a team that had to re-derive parts of a design nobody had kept. So the two most famous objects on this route are among the newest things in the room. The oldest original working digital computer on the site is the Dekatron, a plodding decimal machine that spent its retirement teaching students in Wolverhampton and then sitting in storage, unglamorous enough that nobody ever thought to break it up. Secrecy destroyed the icons and obscurity saved the survivor.

TNMOC is the stop most readers here will care about, and it is not covered by a Bletchley Park ticket. Beyond the Colossus gallery there are rooms of mainframes and minis, and floors of home micros with the keyboards live. Allow a day for the pair of them, then accept that it will be two.

Both entrances are a few minutes on foot from Bletchley station, roughly forty minutes out of London Euston.

## Helmond, where everything is switched on

Almost every machine in the Home Computer Museum is powered up and yours to use, which is the museum's whole argument and not a concession to visitors. Commodore, Atari, MSX, Amiga, early PCs: the shelf you emulated as a teenager, running on the metal the software was written for.

It also operates as a workplace, training and employing people who have struggled to find work elsewhere, largely on restoration and repair, which is why so much of the collection still runs. Helmond sits a short hop east of Eindhoven, so it works as a day trip if you would rather sleep in a bigger town.

## Paderborn, five thousand years in one building

The Heinz Nixdorf MuseumsForum calls itself the largest computer museum in the world, and on floor area the claim holds up. It opens with cuneiform and mechanical calculators and takes its time reaching anything you would recognise as a computer, which is the point: the subject here is five millennia of information technology, and the last fifty years are the final act. It occupies Nixdorf's old headquarters, so the building is an exhibit too. Helmond to Paderborn is the ugly leg, five or six hours with two or three changes, usually through Venlo and the Ruhr.

Start early.

## Lausanne, the last stop

Musée Bolo grew out of one private collection and now holds thousands of machines on the EPFL campus. It is dense and carefully labelled, and it is where the European micros that never crossed the Atlantic get proper shelf space. Paderborn to Lausanne is a full day on the rails, around eight hours via Frankfurt and Basel.

Break it with a night in Basel unless you enjoy suffering.

## Stretching the British end

Britain is the easiest end to extend. London's Science Museum keeps a permanent computing gallery forty minutes down the line from Bletchley, and Cambridge holds the Centre for Computing History, another collection built around machines you are allowed to switch on. Add either and the UK becomes your longest single stretch, which is the point at which an [eSIM UK](https://www.helloroam.com/en-GB/esim-uk) plan at $2.68 a day matters more than the Dutch one.

## Budgeting the rest

Admissions, five rail legs and six to eight nights are the real line items, and rail is the volatile one.
